Fraktale Geometrie

Self-similarity, infinite detail, and simple rules that generate complex structures — a quick tour of fractals on a single page.

Einführung

Fractal geometry studies shapes that repeat their structure at different scales. Unlike classical Euclidean figures, fractals often have non-integer (fractional) dimensions and display self-similarity: zoom in and you keep finding shapes reminiscent of the whole.

Many natural phenomena can be described with fractal ideas, from coastlines and clouds to plants and turbulence. Simple iterative rules — repeatedly applying a transformation — can produce the intricate patterns we call fractals.

  • Self-similarity: parts resemble the whole.
  • Iteration: applying a rule many times.
  • Fractal dimension: a measure between topological dimensions; e.g., the Koch curve has dimension ≈ 1.26.

Beispiele

Koch Snowflake

Start with an equilateral triangle. For each side, replace its middle third with two sides of a smaller equilateral triangle, and repeat. Perimeter grows without bound; area approaches a finite limit.

Iterative rule replace middle third with a "bump"

Sierpiński Triangle

Begin with a filled triangle. Subdivide into four smaller congruent triangles and remove the center. Repeat with each remaining triangle. The result has zero area but infinitely many holes.

Self-similar pieces 3 copies scaled by 1/2

Mandelbrot Set

The set of complex numbers c for which the iteration z ← z² + c (starting at z = 0) does not diverge. Its boundary exhibits endlessly rich, self-similar structure.

Complex dynamics bulbs, filaments, and mini-sets

Natural Patterns

Coastlines, ferns, Romanesco broccoli, and river networks often show fractal-like statistics. Randomized, statistical self-similarity appears across multiple scales.

Nature approximate, not exact, fractals

Fazit

Fractals reveal how complexity can emerge from simple rules. Whether in pure mathematics or in models of nature, they help us describe roughness, branching, and growth across scales. Exploring fractals is an invitation to look closer — and then closer again.
